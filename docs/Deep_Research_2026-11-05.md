# Pivot SkyProof onto drones' existing evidence gap

**Yes, the problem behind SkyProof is real and exists today, but not the version in the current pitch.** What exists, and has data behind it, is this: autonomous delivery and show drones now crash in public at a steady rate, some of those crashes have hurt people, and after every one the only detailed record of what the aircraft sensed and decided sits on the operator's own devices or servers. Investigators, regulators and victims either get what the operator hands over or get nothing. That claim rests on a French BEA report where the logs were missing from all three devices, NTSB cases built on operator-supplied data, a public Amazon–NTSB disagreement over a crash cause, and an Orlando lawsuit spread across four defendants. What does not exist is the part the 0G sponsor flagged. No fielded drone lets an LLM or VLM approve missions, and no official report blames a crash on an AI decision. The record-keeping idea also has plenty of prior art, including Red Cat/GoChain, Southampton/Minima, a Monad hackathon project called Pathfinder Black Box, and India's NPNT. The one part with no prior art found is the bond that pays victims automatically, and that is also the part most open to a "this is just manual judgment" attack. The sponsor was right to call the current pitch solution-first. The fix is to lead with the evidence gap after crashes caused by the automated decisions drones already make, and to pay out only on narrow, objective rule breaches. **Verdict: go, with that reframing.** This report also corrects the earlier evidence pack (`Problem_Evidence_2026-11-05.md`). That pack said none of the crashes injured anyone, which is wrong.

## Drones already crash in public, and some crashes hurt people

The incident record is no longer anecdotal. Amazon's MK30 alone has had about seven events in the US and UK since late 2024. At the Pendleton, Oregon test site in December 2024, **two MK30s "erroneously determined they had touched down due to an incorrect altitude reading from a new software installation"** and lost power in flight. The NTSB investigated, Amazon disputed the conclusion, and deliveries paused in Arizona and Texas in January 2025 ([DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2025/05/19/amazon-delivery-drone-crash-ntsb/); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/amazon-puts-its-drone-deliveries-on-hold-following-two-crash-incidents-140026835.html)). On 1 October 2025 in Tolleson, Arizona, **two MK30s hit the same crane boom within minutes of each other**, and one caught fire. The FAA and NTSB opened investigations, and Amazon resumed flights about two days later after its own internal review ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/02/amazon-drone-crash-faa-ntsb.html); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/ntsb-faa-investigate-2-amazon-delivery-drones-crash-arizona-rcna235345)). After that came a cable strike on lift-off in Waco, Texas in November 2025, which the NTSB declined to investigate ([Flying](https://www.flyingmag.com/ntsb-amazon-drone-delivery-incident-texas/)), an apartment-building strike in Richardson, Texas in February 2026 ([AIAA](https://aiaa.org/2026/02/10/prime-air-drone-falls-in-residential-area-during-texas-test-operations/)), and a crash into a residential garden in Darlington, UK in July 2026. The UK AAIB classified Darlington as an accident, while Amazon said the drone "responded as designed" ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/08/17/amazon-drone-crash-darlington-aaib-caa-verdict/)). Zipline had two parachute descents in Texas in 2026. One landed in the live lanes of Highway 6 near Houston. Zipline said the cause was "under investigation" and later called some answers to safety questions "proprietary" ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/09/16/zipline-answers-drone-safety-questions-amazon-wing-silent/)).

The earlier evidence pack said none of these crashes had injured anyone, and the new notes show that is wrong. At Lake Eola, Orlando, in December 2024, **show drones fell into the crowd and a 7-year-old needed open-heart surgery**. The NTSB final report blamed configuration errors that no cross-check caught ([FOX 35](https://www.fox35orlando.com/news/orlando-drone-show-errors-led-lake-eola-crash-seriously-injured-7-year-old-ntsb)). A Manna delivery drone in Ireland shed a propeller blade that struck a man mowing his lawn, and **the AAIU noted Manna had not notified it as required** ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/dublin/2024/11/08/man-injured-by-fallen-drone-propeller-while-mowing-lawn-investigators-say)). At Barcarès in France, a DJI Inspire 2 filming a festival **injured three people on the ground** ([BEA](https://bea.aero/fileadmin/user_upload/BEA2019-0416_1.pdf)). India has its own case. A Skye Air delivery drone crashed into a Gurugram residential area in February 2024, and police filed an FIR ([ThePrint/PTI](https://theprint.in/india/drone-carrying-package-crashes-in-gurugram-locality-fir-against-company/1969281/)).

Volume is rising fast, so absolute incident counts will rise with it. **Zipline passed 2 million deliveries in January 2026**, and **Walmart passed 1 million US drone deliveries in May 2026, 40% of them in a single quarter** ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/05/31/walmart-1m-drone-deliveries-40/)). On 5 November 2026 Zipline raised $1.5B, and Walmart signed up for 300 more sites ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/11/05/zipline-1-5-billion-raise-walmart-300-sites/)). Aggregate figures exist too: the FAA logged **1,859 drone sighting reports near airports in 2025, up from 1,674** ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/uas/resources/public_records/uas_sightings_report)), and US emergency departments treated **about 4,250 drone injuries between 2015 and 2020** ([UF/IFAS](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/AE560)). Most of those injuries involved consumer drones. Injury data specific to delivery drones barely exists yet, so say so rather than stretch the numbers.

## The record of what happened belongs to the party under investigation

This is the strongest real problem, and it does not need any AI. The best single piece of evidence is the BEA's Barcarès report. The operator blamed a "battery failure" alert. The BEA could not reproduce the fault and found that the post-flight battery values did not match the auto-landing logic. It could not check the operator's account because **"the flight logs were no longer present on the drone, the pilot's tablet or the cameraman's phone"** ([BEA report](https://bea.aero/fileadmin/user_upload/BEA2019-0416_1.pdf)). A national investigator put in writing that it could not verify an operator's story because the logs were gone.

The same pattern runs through US cases. In the 2017 Army helicopter collision with a DJI Phantom over Staten Island, only a motor and an arm of the drone were recovered, and the **operator "provided flight data logs for the incident flight"** ([Unmanned Aerial Online](https://unmanned-aerial.com/ntsb-investigates-drone-helicopter-collision-new-york)). In Orlando, the operator recovered about 42 crashed drones and sent their SD cards to the NTSB, while **about 5 drones went into the lake and their data was lost** ([Unmanned Airspace](https://www.unmannedairspace.info/uncategorized/ntsb-report-into-orlando-drone-accident-finds-multiple-errors/)). At Pendleton, the NTSB and Amazon publicly disagreed about the cause. Bloomberg tied the sensor fault to rain, while the NTSB final report reportedly found no rain that day ([Claims Journal/Bloomberg](https://amp.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/05/19/330673.htm); [Flying](https://www.flyingmag.com/ntsb-amazon-drone-delivery-incident-texas/)). At Tolleson, the operator resumed flying within 48 hours on the strength of its own analysis of its own telemetry, before any public NTSB finding. That is not evidence of wrongdoing. The point is that the record had only one custodian.

The rules do not fix this. EU Regulation 2019/947 only requires the operator to "keep a record" of operations ([legislation.gov.uk](https://legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/947/annex/part/B/adopted/data.pdf)). The UK CAA requires digital records kept for 3 years ([CAA](https://www.caa.co.uk/drones/specific-category/pdra01-operational-authorisation/uas-operator-responsibilities/)). The FAA's pending Part 108 BVLOS rule proposes an operator-run "flight data collection system" and reporting of events above $500 of damage, and the operator can act as its own data service provider ([Inside Unmanned Systems](https://insideunmannedsystems.com/new-bvlos-rule-proposes-comprehensive-pathway-for-routine-operations/)). The research found **no integrity requirement in any of the four regimes checked**. India's March 2021 UAS Rules did mandate tamper-proof NPNT logging, and the liberalised Drone Rules 2021 deferred it ([ORF](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/the-road-ahead-for-drone-regulations-in-india)). That is a regulator once agreeing with SkyProof's thesis and then backing away. The research found no rule that requires tamper-evident drone logs today.

Victims feel this most. The Orlando family's lawsuit names **four defendants: the City of Orlando, Sky Elements, UVify and SPH Engineering**. It argues over geofencing, estimator redundancy, return-to-home and kill-switch design, all of which turn on flight data the defendants hold ([DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2025/08/29/orlando-drone-show-mishap-lawsuit/)). No settlement or ruling had been found eight months or more after the crash. The best analogy outside drones is the Tesla Benavides case. Tesla said it could not find a crash "collision snapshot" that had been uploaded to its own servers. A hacker recovered the data from the car, and the jury found Tesla 33% liable for a **$243M award**. The judge found no sufficient evidence of intent but ordered Tesla to pay the plaintiffs' data-retrieval costs ([Spokesman-Review/WaPo](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2025/aug/29/tesla-said-it-didnt-have-key-data-in-a-fatal-crash)). A hash anchored off the device at crash time would have proved the snapshot existed.

## Evidence-backed versus assumed: where the sponsor's doubt lands

Most of the 0G sponsor's doubt lands on the AI safety officer. Deployed drones do make safety-critical ML decisions. Amazon's MK30 uses ML models trained to recognise people, animals, barriers and aircraft, and decides in flight on evasive manoeuvres ([Aerospace Testing International](https://aerospacetestinginternational.com/?p=21292)). Skydio's X10 plans routes with onboard AI ([Inside Unmanned Systems](https://insideunmannedsystems.com/skydios-autonomy-could-change-the-game-for-public-safety/)). But **no deployed commercial drone was found where an LLM or VLM approves or plans missions**. That work is confined to research prototypes such as UAV-VLA ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05014v2)) and CHAI's simulated landing agents. CHAI is the best proof that this class of decision-maker can be manipulated. A printed "Safe to land" sign made VLM agents judge a **debris-strewn roof safe up to 68.1% of the time in AirSim**, and a fake label hijacked drone tracking **95.5%** of the time ([arXiv 2510.00181](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00181); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/30/road_sign_hijack_ai/)). The awkward consequence is that SkyProof's own AI officer is a VLM judge and is open to the same attack. It should be presented as an advisor whose inputs are logged, not as a trusted approver. The FAA's AI roadmap says the same thing plainly: **"responsibility … rests with the system designer and AI developer, not the AI itself"** ([AIN](https://backend.ainonline.com/aviation-news/futureflight/2024-08-21/faa-issues-first-artificial-intelligence-roadmap)).

The Pathfinder Black Box hackathon project matters most here. It already hash-chains an AI copilot's decisions once per second on-chain, but it has no bond or payout. Its README admits it proves the log was not altered, not that the sensors told the truth. Judges will make the same "garbage in, garbage out" point against SkyProof, so answer it before they ask. Insurance is also already mandatory in the EU, the UK (commercial flights) and India (non-nano drones), so the bond cannot be pitched as filling a "no insurance" gap there ([EASA](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/node/119281); [UK CAA](https://www.caa.co.uk/drones/open-category/moving-on-to-more-advanced-flying/insurance-requirements/)). What it can claim is speed and neutral evidence: a fast first payout to the victim ahead of the insurer, triggered by an objective breach.

## Reframe: an independent black box for decisions drones already make

The pitch that matches the evidence goes like this. Drones already make automated, safety-critical decisions: detect-and-avoid, auto-landing, parachute deployment, and "responded as designed" logic. When those decisions go wrong, the only record belongs to the operator. SkyProof makes that record tamper-evident from the moment it is written and keeps it out of the operator's sole control. The AI officer then becomes a forward-looking extra. VLM agents are coming (CHAI, UAV-VLA), CHAI shows they can be hijacked, so their inputs and outputs must be on the record too. The bond moves from "the AI judges fault" to **objective triggers checked against a signed permission**. Did the drone leave its permitted geofence or altitude band? Did it fly over a declared no-fly or crowd zone? Is the hash chain broken or missing for the incident window? India's NPNT permission artefact is the right model to name. It is a DGCA-signed mission permission that drone firmware checks before arming ([FactorDaily](https://archive.factordaily.com/indian-digital-sky-system-for-drones-takes-shape/)). Each of these triggers can be computed from the record, and none needs a human to decide fault. That answers the RSM critique. The multi-party fault fight in a case like Orlando stays with the courts, but the courts now get a record that nobody could edit.

## Conclusion

**Go, but pitch it the other way round.** Start with Barcarès and Orlando, which give the judges an injured victim and an investigator who could not verify the operator's story. Then show that the drones flying today make automated decisions whose only record belongs to the operator, and that the rules still do not demand anything better. The AI safety officer becomes a short "and when VLM agents arrive, CHAI shows why their inputs must be on record too" line, rather than the headline. The minimum build should show four things live. First, a simulated flight streaming hash-chained decision records (sensor snapshot, autopilot or AI action, reason) to 0G Storage, with periodic anchors on 0G Chain. Second, a signed NPNT-style permission listing geofence, altitude ceiling and no-fly zones. Third, a CRE job that checks the record against that permission and against chain continuity. Fourth, an Arc USDC bond that pays out only on one of those objective breaches. The demo should break the chain or cross the geofence on stage and show the payout. Put the BEA quote on the first slide.

For comparison, a separate RedZone evidence report exists at `D:/Projects/redzone/reports/`. It was not read for this report, so the two problem cases are not weighed against each other here. SkyProof's evidence is real and has the firmer institutional backing (investigator reports, an NTSB final report, a lawsuit), but almost all of it supports the record and custody problem rather than the AI-approval layer. Whichever project the team picks, SkyProof only holds up in front of judges if it is presented as a black box for the decisions drones already make, not as an AI-approval layer.
